\subsection{Schedule-free averaging}
\label{app:training-sf}

The optimiser is the schedule-free method of \citet{defazio2024road} with the changes of
SF-NorMuon \citep{apte2026anytime} and ScheduleFree+ \citep{defazio2026schedulefreeplus}. It
keeps a fast sequence $\vartheta_t$, which takes the base optimiser's updates, and a slow average
$\bar\vartheta_t$ of it. The gradient is taken at an interpolation $\tilde\vartheta_t$ of the
two, so the network is trained at $\tilde\vartheta_t$ and evaluated at $\bar\vartheta_t$. At step
$t = 1, 2, \dots$, with $u_t$ the base update (\cref{app:training-base}) from the gradient at
$\tilde\vartheta_{t-1}$,
\begin{align}
  \vartheta_t &= \vartheta_{t-1} + u_t - \gamma_{t-1}\varkappa\, \vartheta_{t-1}, \label{eq:training-z}\\
  \bar\vartheta_t &= (1 - c_t)\, \bar\vartheta_{t-1} + c_t\, \vartheta_t,
  \qquad c_t = \frac{w_t}{\sum_{s=T_0+1}^{t} w_s},
  \qquad w_t = t^r\,\bar\gamma_{t-1}^2 \quad (t > T_0), \label{eq:training-x}\\
  \tilde\vartheta_t &= \varrho_t\, \bar\vartheta_t + (1 - \varrho_t)\, \vartheta_t, \label{eq:training-y}\\
  \varrho_t &= \varrho_0 + (\varrho_1 - \varrho_0)\,
  \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{\log\left(\max(t, T_0)/T_0\right)}{\log(T/T_0)},\, 0,\, 1\right),
  \label{eq:training-beta}
\end{align}
with $\bar\vartheta_t = \vartheta_t$ for $t \le T_0$, $T$ the run's total steps, and $\gamma_t$,
$\bar\gamma_t$ the Muon learning rate with and without the optional cooldown
(\cref{app:training-schedules}).

\paragraph{Why schedule-free.}
The averaged weights $\bar\vartheta_t$ are usable at every step, and \citet{defazio2024road}
report that they match or beat a tuned cosine or linear-decay schedule. A run can therefore be
stopped, evaluated and extended without waiting for a schedule to end. The guarantee is weaker
here than in the original method, because the ramp of \cref{eq:training-beta} (and the cooldown,
if used) depends on $T$: extending a run changes $\varrho_t$ from then on, though every earlier
update stands.

\paragraph{Averaging.}
For the first $T_0$ steps, \TrainingWarmStartWord{} warmup lengths, $\bar\vartheta$ follows
$\vartheta$; averaging starts after this warm start, so the early iterates never enter the average
\citep{defazio2026schedulefreeplus}. The weights are ScheduleFree+'s $t^r\bar\gamma^2$ with
$r = \TrainingAveragePower{}$. Since $T_0$ outlasts the warmup, $\bar\gamma$ is constant while
averaging, and $\bar\vartheta_t = \sum_{s>T_0} s^r \vartheta_s / \sum_{s>T_0} s^r$: later iterates
count more, so the average forgets the start of a long run faster than a uniform one. A cooldown
would not change these weights, which use the uncooled rate $\bar\gamma$.

\paragraph{Interpolation.}
$\varrho_t$ stays at $\varrho_0 = \TrainingBetaStart{}$ through the warm start and rises
log-linearly to $\varrho_1 = \TrainingBetaEnd{}$ at the last step.
\citet{defazio2026schedulefreeplus} report that a low $\varrho$ converges faster early and a high
one ends lower; SF-NorMuon keeps $\varrho$ constant at $\varrho_0$ \citep{apte2026anytime}.

\paragraph{Weight decay on $\vartheta$.}
Muon's updates have a fixed size whatever the gradient's, so without decay the weights grow
\citep{liu2025muon}; inside schedule-free the growth is in $\vartheta$. On an earlier version of
this network trained without decay, $\vartheta$ drifted out of the basin of good networks while
only $\tilde\vartheta$ stayed good. Decay on $\vartheta$ keeps every iterate bounded
\citep{apte2026anytime}; it applies only to the matrices Muon updates. On the same earlier
network, decaying the AdamW parameters shrank the head's output rows for one coordinate to a small
fraction of the others, so AdamW runs without decay.

\paragraph{What is not used.}
ScheduleFree+'s Polyak step scales each update by $L(y) - L^\ast$ with $L^\ast = 0$
\citep{defazio2026schedulefreeplus}. The flow-matching loss has a nonzero floor (\cref{app:fm}),
so this step would track the whole loss rather than its excess and would not shrink near the
optimum. Its inverse gradient-norm weighting is left out too, so the step stays the set learning
rate.

\paragraph{Strength of the evidence.}
Schedule-free averaging \citep{defazio2024road} and Muon \citep{jordan2024muon,liu2025muon} are
established; NorMuon's row normalisation \citep{li2025normuon} rests on one study. Inner momentum
inside schedule-free, decay on $\vartheta$, the warm start, the $t^r\bar\gamma^2$ weights and the
$\varrho$ ramp come from two preprints \citep{apte2026anytime,defazio2026schedulefreeplus} on
language models. None was tested on flow matching, and none was ablated here.

\subsection{Base optimisers}
\label{app:training-base}

The gradient of the loss (\cref{eq:loss}) is clipped to a global norm of \TrainingClipNorm{}. The
parameters are then split between Muon \citep{jordan2024muon} and AdamW, and $u_t$ in
\cref{eq:training-z} concatenates their updates.

\paragraph{Muon with NorMuon rows.}
For a weight matrix $W \in \R^{m \times n}$ (output by input) with clipped gradient $G$, Muon
keeps a momentum $M \leftarrow \upsilon M + (1-\upsilon)\, G$, takes the Nesterov look-ahead
$N = \upsilon M + (1-\upsilon)\, G$, and replaces it by an approximation $O$ of the nearest
semi-orthogonal matrix $UV\T$ (from $N = U\Sigma V\T$), computed with
\TrainingNewtonSchulzWord{} quintic Newton--Schulz iterations \citep{jordan2024muon}. The update
$-\gamma_t\, \TrainingMuonRms{}\sqrt{\max(m, n)}\; O$ has an RMS of $\TrainingMuonRms{}\gamma_t$
for every shape, the scale at which \citet{liu2025muon} match AdamW's update size, so one learning
rate serves every matrix. NorMuon \citep{li2025normuon} then evens out the rows: with
$\hat u = u / \operatorname{RMS}(u)$ and a per-row second moment
$v_i \leftarrow \TrainingNorMuonBeta{}\, v_i + (1-\TrainingNorMuonBeta{})\, \tfrac1n \sum_j \hat u_{ij}^2$,
\begin{equation}
  u_{ij} \leftarrow \frac{\hat u_{ij}}{\sqrt{v_i} + \epsilon}\,
  \frac{\lVert u \rVert_F}{\lVert \hat u / (\sqrt{v} + \epsilon) \rVert_F},
  \label{eq:training-normuon}
\end{equation}
which keeps the Frobenius norm, and so Muon's RMS. $v$ needs no bias correction, since it cancels
in the rescaling. Weight matrices stacked over the backbone's blocks are orthogonalised one block
at a time.

\paragraph{AdamW.}
Every other parameter is updated by AdamW with Nesterov momentum. Its first moment is float32,
because a bfloat16 one loses the small increments of a smooth gradient. Both base optimisers keep
their momentum inside schedule-free: the original method drops it \citep{defazio2024road}, and
SF-NorMuon and ScheduleFree+ reinstate it \citep{apte2026anytime,defazio2026schedulefreeplus}.

\paragraph{Which parameters Muon takes.}
Muon is meant for hidden-layer matrices \citep{jordan2024muon}. It takes the weight matrix of
every linear layer in \TrainingMuonFieldCount{} modules of the network (\cref{app:network}): the
backbone, the two conditioning \acp{mlp} (of the time and centre frequency, and of the data
stream's frequency), the two Fourier-feature \acp{mlp} (of the source frequency and of the data
tokens' frequency bins) and the output modulation, plus the head's hidden layer. AdamW takes the
rest: the piecewise-linear embedding of the sources, the linear embedding of the data tokens,
the attention-sink embeddings, the query--key gains, every bias and the head's output layer. Muon holds \qty{\TrainingMuonShareXS}{\percent} of the
parameters of full-XS, \qty{\TrainingMuonShareB}{\percent} of full-B and \qty{\TrainingMuonShareXL}{\percent} of full-XL.

\paragraph{Learning rates.}
The peak rates, \num{\TrainingMuonRate} for Muon and \num{\TrainingAdamRate} for AdamW, were not tuned
by a sweep on this network, and neither was the batch.

\subsection{Schedules}
\label{app:training-schedules}

Both learning rates rise linearly from zero over the first \qty{\TrainingWarmupPercent}{\percent} of the run
and then stay constant (\cref{tab:training-recipe}). Schedule-free needs the warmup
\citep{defazio2024road}; after it, the average $\bar\vartheta$ does the work of a decaying
schedule. A linear cooldown to zero over a final fraction of the run is available but off; it
would act on $\vartheta$'s steps and on the decay, not on the averaging weights
(\cref{app:training-sf}).

The step counts are a wall-time budget, not a convergence criterion: size B trains in about a day
on one A100 with the bfloat16 backbone, \qty{\TrainingComputeNarrowBHours}{\hour} of steps on the narrow
band and \qty{\TrainingComputeFullBHours}{\hour} on the full band (\cref{fig:training-compute}a).

\subsection{Batch}
\label{app:training-batch}

A batch holds \TrainingBatch{} $(x_0, t)$ samples: \TrainingObservations{} simulated
observations, each with its own centre frequency $\fw$ and sources (\cref{app:priors}), and
\TrainingDraws{} draws of the base point and time per observation. The data stream of an
observation is encoded once and shared by its draws (\cref{app:network}), so the draws amortise
both the simulation and the encoding. The times are stratified over the whole batch, one per
stratum of \TrainingBatch{} equal strata (\cref{app:fm-clock}).

Every batch is simulated on the device inside the step, so no observation is seen twice. This is
what keeps the simulator cheap (\cref{fig:training-overhead}a,b): it takes
\qty{\TrainingComputeNarrowBDrawPercent}{\percent} of narrow-B's A100 step and
\qty{\TrainingComputeFullBDrawPercent}{\percent} of full-B's, while on \TrainingDrawCpuCores{} CPU cores the
full-band batch alone takes \qty{\TrainingDrawCpuFullSeconds}{\second}, \TrainingDrawCpuOverStep{} times
full-B's whole A100 step.

The gradient can be accumulated over equal micro-batches of whole observations, which holds one
micro-batch's activations at a time and leaves the mean gradient unchanged; by default the batch
is not split. Because the batch is simulated before it is split, splitting barely lowers the peak
memory on the full band, where the simulated batch alone plans \qty{\TrainingDrawPlanFullGib}{\gibi\byte}
(\cref{fig:training-compute}c).

\subsection{Training loop, checkpoints and resume}
\label{app:training-loop}

\paragraph{Epochs.}
An epoch is \TrainingEpochSteps{} steps (\TrainingEpochsNarrow{} epochs on the narrow band,
\TrainingEpochsFull{} on the full band) compiled as one program: the simulator, the loss, the
gradient and the optimiser update of all its steps run in one loop on the device.

\paragraph{Eval-catalog loss.}
After every epoch, the \EvaluationInjections{} eval injections with their noise
(\cref{app:evaluation-injections}), each at the midpoints of \FlowMatchingLossBins{} equal steps
in $s$, give the loss of \cref{eq:loss} at $\bar\vartheta$ and at $\tilde\vartheta$; the same
injections at the midpoints of the \FlowMatchingLossBins{} bins of $\log_{10}(1-s)$ down to
$10^{\FlowMatchingLossDecades}$ give, per bin and coordinate group, $\tilde\vartheta$'s squared
error of $\hat X_1$, its predicted $\sigma^2$ and their ratio (\cref{app:fm-loss}). The catalog and
its draws of $x_0$, from a random stream that training never uses, are fixed for the run, so the
curves move only with the weights. It also records the RMS of $\bar\vartheta$'s
backbone weights and the RMS of $\vartheta - \bar\vartheta$, which flags a fast sequence running
away from the average (\cref{app:training-sf}). Training stops before the checkpoint if any loss
of the epoch is not finite, so the last checkpoint is always finite.

\paragraph{Checkpoints and resume.}
Every epoch saves, in the background while the next epoch trains, the training weights
$\tilde\vartheta$, the optimiser state (which holds $\vartheta$, $\bar\vartheta$, the base
optimisers' moments and the averaging weight sum), the random key and the loss histories, keeping
only the latest. Evaluation reads $\bar\vartheta$ from the optimiser state, which is why the
checkpoint always keeps it. A checkpoint is \qty{\TrainingCheckpointNarrowBGib}{\gibi\byte} on narrow-B and \qty{\TrainingCheckpointFullBGib}{\gibi\byte} on full-B, and
the save blocks training for \qty{\TrainingCheckpointNarrowBBlock}{\second} on narrow-B and
\qty{\TrainingCheckpointFullBBlock}{\second} on full-B. A job runs for at most \qty{\TrainingJobHours}{\hour} and
requeues itself \TrainingRequeueMinutes{} minutes before its limit; the next job resumes from the
latest checkpoint after a restart of about \qty{\TrainingCheckpointNarrowBRestartMinutes}{\minute} (narrow-B)
and \qty{\TrainingCheckpointFullBRestartMinutes}{\minute} (full-B), mostly compilation; full-B's save, restart and loss
are measured on an A100 (\qty{40}{\giga\byte} PCIe) running nothing else, narrow-B's on an A100 (\qty{80}{\giga\byte}). Because the key
is checkpointed, a resumed run draws the same batches as an uninterrupted one; only the epoch cut
off by the limit is repeated. A resume runs the commit the run was started from and may change only
the micro-batch split, the step count and evaluation settings; raising the step count extends the
run (\cref{app:training-sf}). With the default epoch, a job loses
\qty{\TrainingCheckpointNarrowBLostPercent}{\percent} (narrow-B) and \qty{\TrainingCheckpointFullBLostPercent}{\percent}
(full-B) of its wall time to anything but saved steps; the best epoch lengths,
\TrainingCheckpointNarrowBBestInterval{} and \TrainingCheckpointFullBBestInterval{} steps, would
lose \qty{\TrainingCheckpointNarrowBBestLostPercent}{\percent} and \qty{\TrainingCheckpointFullBBestLostPercent}{\percent}
(\cref{fig:training-overhead}c).

\subsection{Precision}
\label{app:training-precision}

Parameters, $\vartheta$, $\bar\vartheta$ and AdamW's moments are float32. By default the backbone
runs in bfloat16, forward and backward, from bfloat16 copies of its float32 weights, while the
embeddings, the conditioning \acp{mlp} and the head stay in float32 (\cref{app:network}); Muon's
momentum and orthogonalisation run in bfloat16 (\cref{app:training-base}). The bfloat16 backbone
makes a step \numrange{\TrainingBackboneSpeedupAMin}{\TrainingBackboneSpeedupAMax} times faster than
float32 on an A100 and \numrange{\TrainingBackboneSpeedupLMin}{\TrainingBackboneSpeedupLMax} times on an
L40S, larger for the larger sizes; bfloat16 attention alone gains at most
\qty{\TrainingAttentionGainPercent}{\percent} (\cref{fig:training-compute}b).

A float32 backbone is available, but it needs the GPU's matrix-multiplication autotuning switched
off and full float32 matrix products (no TF32). On an earlier version of the network, float32
programs compiled with autotuning gave silently wrong, over-confident posteriors on an A100 and on
a laptop GPU, while the same program without autotuning reproduced the CPU. This was not re-tested
on the current network; the bfloat16 backbone was not affected.

\subsection{Compute}
\label{app:training-compute}

\Cref{fig:training-compute} gives the measured cost of every band and size of \cref{app:network}
at the default batch, on an A100 (\qty{80}{\giga\byte}) and an L40S. The narrow band's step is the
network's. The full band simulates its observations at many more frequency points (\cref{app:forward-model}), a fixed cost per batch
that weighs most on the small sizes (\cref{fig:training-overhead}a) and sets a memory floor
(\cref{fig:training-compute}c). In the step benchmark every size fits unsplit on the A100, and
full-XL needs two micro-batches on the L40S; with the trainer's eval-catalog pass and checkpointing
alongside, narrow-XL on the L40S and full-XL on the A100 (\qty{80}{\giga\byte}) need a split
too, and full-XL does not fit the L40S even split.
