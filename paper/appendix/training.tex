\subsection{Optimiser}
\label{app:training-optimiser}

The gradient of the loss (\cref{eq:loss}) is clipped to a global norm of \TrainingClipNorm{}. The
parameters are then split between Muon \citep{jordan2024muon} and AdamW, with the settings with
which \citet{liu2025muon} scale Muon to large language models.

\paragraph{Muon.}
For a weight matrix $W \in \R^{m \times n}$ (output by input) with clipped gradient $G$, Muon
keeps a momentum $M \leftarrow \upsilon M + (1-\upsilon)\, G$, takes the Nesterov look-ahead
$N = \upsilon M + (1-\upsilon)\, G$, and replaces it by an approximation $O$ of the nearest
semi-orthogonal matrix $UV\T$ (from $N = U\Sigma V\T$), computed with
\TrainingNewtonSchulzWord{} quintic Newton--Schulz iterations \citep{jordan2024muon}. The step is
\begin{equation}
  W \leftarrow W - \gamma_t \left(\TrainingMuonRms{}\sqrt{\max(m, n)}\; O + \varkappa W\right).
  \label{eq:training-muon}
\end{equation}
Its orthogonal part has an RMS of $\TrainingMuonRms{}\gamma_t$ for every shape, the scale at which
\citet{liu2025muon} match AdamW's update size, so one learning rate serves every matrix. Weight
matrices stacked over the backbone's blocks are orthogonalised one block at a time.

\paragraph{Weight decay.}
Muon's updates have a fixed size whatever the gradient's, so without decay the weights grow and
Muon's lead over AdamW fades on long runs \citep{liu2025muon}. The decoupled decay of
\cref{eq:training-muon}, $\varkappa = \TrainingWeightDecay{}$ as in \citet{liu2025muon}, applies
only to the matrices Muon updates, and through $\gamma_t$ it ends with the cooldown. On an earlier
version of this network, decaying the AdamW parameters shrank the head's output rows for one
coordinate to a small fraction of the others, so AdamW runs without decay.

\paragraph{AdamW.}
Every other parameter is updated by AdamW. Its first moment is float32, because a bfloat16 one
loses the small increments of a smooth gradient.

\paragraph{Which parameters Muon takes.}
Muon is meant for hidden-layer matrices \citep{jordan2024muon}. It takes the weight matrix of
every linear layer in \TrainingMuonFieldCount{} modules of the network (\cref{app:network}): the
backbone, the two conditioning \acp{mlp} (of the time, and of the window's range of $f_0$), the
two Fourier-feature \acp{mlp} (of the source frequency and of the data tokens' frequency bins) and
the output modulations of the flow and of the frame, plus the head's hidden layer. AdamW takes the
rest: the linear embeddings of the sources and of the data tokens, the frame-register and
attention-sink embeddings, the frame's output layer, the query--key gains, every bias and the head's output layer. Muon holds \qty{\TrainingMuonShareXS}{\percent} of the
parameters of full-XS, \qty{\TrainingMuonShareB}{\percent} of full-B and \qty{\TrainingMuonShareXL}{\percent} of full-XL.

\paragraph{Learning rates.}
The peak rates, \num{\TrainingMuonRate} for Muon and \num{\TrainingAdamRate} for AdamW, were not tuned
by a sweep on this network, and neither was the batch. Muon's matches AdamW's because its update
RMS does \citep{liu2025muon}.
At these rates five of the six runs of \cref{app:results} trained to their end with every loss
finite. Full-band $S=4$ did not: from a few epochs before, isolated single steps sent the frame's
loss and the gradient norm up by many decades while the epoch medians stayed flat, and at epoch
\ResultsFullFourDivergedEpoch, just before its cooldown, a loss turned non-finite
(\cref{app:results-runs}). The same run's forks, cooled from earlier snapshots, are unaffected. No
rate was swept, so whether lower rates avoid such spikes, or higher ones train faster, is open.

\paragraph{Strength of the evidence.}
Muon \citep{jordan2024muon}, its decay and update scale \citep{liu2025muon} and the
warmup--stable--decay schedule \citep{hu2024minicpm,hagele2024scaling} are established on
language models. None was tested on flow matching, and none was ablated here.

\subsection{Schedule}
\label{app:training-schedules}

Both learning rates rise linearly from zero over the first \qty{\TrainingWarmupPercent}{\percent}
of the run, stay constant, and fall to zero over the last \qty{\TrainingCooldownPercent}{\percent}
as $\gamma_t = \gamma\,\bigl(1 - \sqrt{(t - T_c)/(T - T_c)}\bigr)$, with $T$ the run's total steps
and $T_c$ the cooldown's start (\cref{tab:training-recipe}). This is the warmup--stable--decay
schedule \citep{hu2024minicpm} with the $1-\sqrt{\cdot}$ cooldown that \citet{hagele2024scaling}
find best; with a cooldown of a fifth of the run, they report it matching a cosine schedule.
Only the cooldown depends on $T$, so raising $T$ before the cooldown starts extends the run as if
it had been planned that long.

The step counts are a wall-time budget, not a convergence criterion: size B trains in about a day
on one A100 with the bfloat16 backbone, \qty{\TrainingComputeNarrowBHours}{\hour} of steps on the narrow
band and \qty{\TrainingComputeFullBHours}{\hour} on the full band (\cref{fig:training-compute}a).
The runs of \cref{app:results} were sized the same way, from the epoch time of a
\qty{30}{\minute} smoke test of each band and source count, to \ResultsEpochs{} epochs: about
\ResultsNarrowHours\,h on the narrow band and \ResultsFullHours\,h on the full band (A100 hours of
steps, without restarts). A later cooldown ends lower (\cref{tab:results-forks}): from forks at epoch 4
to epoch 32 the eval-catalogue loss falls in every configuration, except full-band $S=4$ at epoch 16,
whose snapshot caught a noisy epoch. Cooling at the planned epoch, after \ResultsEpochs{} epochs, still
gains on narrow $S=4$ and full $S=8$, which are therefore not converged, but not on narrow $S=2$ and
$S=8$, where the fork from epoch 32 ends below the main run.

\subsection{Batch}
\label{app:training-batch}

A batch holds \TrainingBatch{} $(Z_0, t)$ samples: \TrainingObservations{} simulated
observations, each with its own centre frequency $\fw$ and sources (\cref{app:priors}), and
\TrainingDraws{} draws of the base point and time per observation. The data stream of an
observation is encoded once and shared by its draws (\cref{app:network}), so the draws amortise
both the simulation and the encoding. The times are stratified over the whole batch, one per
stratum of \TrainingBatch{} equal strata (\cref{app:fm-clock}).

Every batch is simulated on the device inside the step, so no observation is seen twice. This is
what keeps the simulator cheap (\cref{fig:training-overhead}a,b): it takes
\qty{\TrainingComputeNarrowBDrawPercent}{\percent} of narrow-B's A100 step and
\qty{\TrainingComputeFullBDrawPercent}{\percent} of full-B's, while on \TrainingDrawCpuCores{} CPU cores the
full-band batch alone takes \qty{\TrainingDrawCpuFullSeconds}{\second}, \TrainingDrawCpuOverStep{} times
full-B's whole A100 step.

The gradient can be accumulated over equal micro-batches of whole observations, which holds one
micro-batch's activations at a time and leaves the mean gradient unchanged; by default the batch
is not split. Because the batch is simulated before it is split, splitting barely lowers the peak
memory on the full band, where the simulated batch alone plans \qty{\TrainingDrawPlanFullGib}{\gibi\byte}
(\cref{fig:training-compute}c).

\subsection{Training loop, checkpoints and resume}
\label{app:training-loop}

\paragraph{Epochs.}
An epoch is \TrainingEpochSteps{} steps (\TrainingEpochsNarrow{} epochs on the narrow band,
\TrainingEpochsFull{} on the full band) compiled as one program: the simulator, the loss, the
gradient and the optimiser update of all its steps run in one loop on the device.

\paragraph{Eval-catalogue loss.}
After every epoch, the \EvaluationInjections{} eval injections with their noise
(\cref{app:evaluation-injections}), each at the midpoints of \FlowMatchingLossBins{} equal steps
in $s$, give the frame's and the flow's negative log-likelihoods; the same
injections at the midpoints of the \FlowMatchingLossBins{} bins of $\log_{10}(1-s)$ down to
$10^{\FlowMatchingLossDecades}$ give, per bin and coordinate group, the predicted $\log_{10}\tau$ and the
squared standardised error $((Z_1-\hat Z_1)/\tau)^2$ (\cref{app:fm-loss}), and per bin of the
source's \ac{snr} the frame's $\log_{10}\sigma/\sigma_\pi$, its $\kappa^2$ and the width of $f_0$
over its Fisher width (\cref{app:fm-frame}). The catalog and its draws of $Z_0$, from a random stream that training never uses, are fixed for the run, so the
curves move only with the weights. It also records the RMS of the backbone weights, which the
decay of \cref{eq:training-muon} holds in check. Training stops before the checkpoint if any loss
of the epoch is not finite, so the last checkpoint is always finite.

\paragraph{Checkpoints and resume.}
Every epoch saves, in the background while the next epoch trains, the weights, the optimisers'
moments, the random key and the loss histories, keeping only the latest. A second checkpoint keeps
the epoch with the lowest sum of the eval-catalogue frame and flow negative log-likelihoods
(\cref{app:evaluation-weights}). A checkpoint is \qty{\TrainingCheckpointNarrowBGib}{\gibi\byte} on narrow-B and \qty{\TrainingCheckpointFullBGib}{\gibi\byte} on full-B, and
the save blocks training for \qty{\TrainingCheckpointNarrowBBlock}{\second} on narrow-B and
\qty{\TrainingCheckpointFullBBlock}{\second} on full-B. A job runs for at most \qty{\TrainingJobHours}{\hour} and
requeues itself \TrainingRequeueMinutes{} minutes before its limit; the next job resumes from the
latest checkpoint after a restart of about \qty{\TrainingCheckpointNarrowBRestartMinutes}{\minute} (narrow-B)
and \qty{\TrainingCheckpointFullBRestartMinutes}{\minute} (full-B), mostly compilation; full-B's save, restart and loss
are measured on an A100 (\qty{40}{\giga\byte} PCIe) running nothing else, narrow-B's on an A100 (\qty{80}{\giga\byte}). Because the key
is checkpointed, a resumed run draws the same batches as an uninterrupted one; only the epoch cut
off by the limit is repeated. A resume runs the commit the run was started from and may change only
the micro-batch split, the step count and evaluation settings; raising the step count before the
cooldown extends the run (\cref{app:training-schedules}). With the default epoch, a job loses
\qty{\TrainingCheckpointNarrowBLostPercent}{\percent} (narrow-B) and \qty{\TrainingCheckpointFullBLostPercent}{\percent}
(full-B) of its wall time to anything but saved steps; the best epoch lengths,
\TrainingCheckpointNarrowBBestInterval{} and \TrainingCheckpointFullBBestInterval{} steps, would
lose \qty{\TrainingCheckpointNarrowBBestLostPercent}{\percent} and \qty{\TrainingCheckpointFullBBestLostPercent}{\percent}
(\cref{fig:training-overhead}c).
\paragraph{Snapshots and cooldown forks.}
Every \ResultsSnapshotEvery{} epochs a run also keeps a snapshot of its whole state, which the
rolling checkpoint overwrites. A fork restarts from the snapshot at epoch $k$ with the total cut to
$k/(1-c)$ epochs, $c$ the cooldown fraction, so it cools down at once, over $ck/(1-c)$ epochs, as a run
planned that long from the start would have; the schedule before the cooldown depends only on the
warmup, which every fork shares with its parent. Forks from epochs \ResultsForkEpochs{} of each run
give, at the cost of the cooldowns alone, the loss and the posteriors of four shorter runs
(\cref{tab:results-forks}).

\paragraph{The runs on the cluster.}
The runs of \cref{app:results} ran on single A100s of a shared cluster, the narrow and the full band
each on its own GPU, their source counts one after another, while a third GPU ran the forks and
evaluations as their snapshots appeared. Jobs of \TrainingJobHours{}\,h requeued themselves and
resumed as above.
Measured on the cluster, the narrow-band runs took \ResultsWallNarrow{}\,h of wall time with
\ResultsRequeuesNarrow{} requeue each, and the full-band runs that finished \ResultsWallFull{}\,h with
\ResultsRequeuesFull{}; the full-band run with $S=4$ diverged after \ResultsWallFullFour{}\,h
(\cref{app:results-runs}).

\subsection{Precision}
\label{app:training-precision}

Parameters and AdamW's moments are float32. By default the backbone
runs in bfloat16, forward and backward, from bfloat16 copies of its float32 weights, while the
embeddings, the conditioning \acp{mlp} and the head stay in float32 (\cref{app:network}); Muon's
momentum and orthogonalisation run in bfloat16 (\cref{app:training-optimiser}). The bfloat16 backbone
makes a step \numrange{\TrainingBackboneSpeedupAMin}{\TrainingBackboneSpeedupAMax} times faster than
float32 on an A100 and \numrange{\TrainingBackboneSpeedupLMin}{\TrainingBackboneSpeedupLMax} times on an
L40S, larger for the larger sizes; bfloat16 attention alone gains at most
\qty{\TrainingAttentionGainPercent}{\percent} (\cref{fig:training-compute}b).

A float32 backbone is available, but it needs the GPU's matrix-multiplication autotuning switched
off and full float32 matrix products (no TF32). On an earlier version of the network, float32
programs compiled with autotuning gave silently wrong, over-confident posteriors on an A100 and on
a laptop GPU, while the same program without autotuning reproduced the CPU. This was not re-tested
on the current network; the bfloat16 backbone was not affected.

\subsection{Compute}
\label{app:training-compute}

\Cref{fig:training-compute} gives the measured cost of every band and size of \cref{app:network}
at the default batch, on an A100 (\qty{80}{\giga\byte}) and an L40S. The narrow band's step is the
network's. The full band simulates its observations at many more frequency points (\cref{app:forward-model}), a fixed cost per batch
that weighs most on the small sizes (\cref{fig:training-overhead}a) and sets a memory floor
(\cref{fig:training-compute}c). In the step benchmark every size fits unsplit on the A100, and
full-XL needs two micro-batches on the L40S; with the trainer's eval-catalogue pass and checkpointing
alongside, narrow-XL on the L40S and full-XL on the A100 (\qty{80}{\giga\byte}) need a split
too, and full-XL does not fit the L40S even split.
